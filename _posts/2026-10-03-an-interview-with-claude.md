I was recently asked to participate with a survey by Claude, answering questions 
by the ["Anthropic Interviewer"](https://www.anthropic.com/about-anthropic-interviewer).

Because I cannot half ass writing, I gave way too long, way too detailed answers 
to each and every question. To make the experience worth while, I decided to

> **Claude: I'm an AI interviewer from Anthropic. As AI becoming more capable and more consequential, we want to hear how it's showing up in your life. Please don't share anything private such as health details, personal information, or identifiable information, about others.**
> 
> **Ready to start?**

Yes.

> **Great. To start, what's the last thing you used AI for?**

I'm a doctoral student. I was using to write some simulation code. The simulation was working fine (so it seemed), but I wanted to understand the code and I was struggling with that. I think 1 spent hour just reading the code, trying to understand it.

> **That's an interesting situation - the code working but you still wanting to understand it yourself. What made you spend that hour reading rather than just moving forward with the working output?**

Lack of trust, hard time delegating. Feeling like its my job to actually understand how and why it worked. Plus, as a student learning is part of the job. 

> **That makes sense -- the sense that understanding the mechanism is part of what you're actually there to do. Thanks for that.**
> 
> **Now, thinking more broadly: can you tell me about yor most memorable moment with AI?**

I think my "moment of shock", the moment when I first internallzed ["the bitter lesson"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bitter_lesson) was the news of the [Jacobian Theorem counter-example](https://www.smithsonianmag.com/smart-news/ai-disproves-a-decades-old-mathematical-idea-the-biggest-conjecture-that-the-tech-has-played-a-role-in-yet-180989189/). I remember reading about "What AI will do to Math" a few years ago and dismissing it.

In my own personal use, I've been impressed with how good its gotten. But there isn't a single "memorable" moment. I guess there was one time when I used I is a "google" to try and find a podcast episode where something very specific happened. Its something that google would not be able to find, too much of the anecdote involved "unrelated keywords". This anecdote comes to mind though I dont think its particularly important or impressive.

> **The Jacobian conjecture counter-example is past my knowledge cutoff, so I don't know the details of that news but I'd like to hear more about your reaction to it. What was it about that particular result that shifted something for you, given you'd dismissed the earlier predictions?**

I assumed math was a discipline that required too much precision and that LLMs would always, inherently, be prone to hallucinations. Even if the rate of hallucinations went down, a single mistake in a long chain of reasoning might be enough to ruin a math problem.

But AI has gotten good at math and was able to show some math theorem counter-examples. After the Jacobian one, there were others still. But the Jacobian one was the first one that impressed me.

> **So it undercut the specific reason you'd had for thinking math was safe from this -- that a single error anywhere in the chain would be fatal. What did that shift mean for you personally, in terms of your own work or how you think about your path?**

It broke my intuition of what the limlts of his technology were. Im now not sure how much better it can get. This really unsettled my vision of what the future could look like. Both for the world and me personally.

In my personal life, I've just kept doing my job as I would normally, even if I'm less sure it will be of any value to anyone. Maybe just trying to use AI a little bit more for coding.

> **Thank you for being candid about that -- the uncertain: about whether the work will be of value to anyone is significant thing to sit with.**
> 
> **Setting that alongside everything else, tell me about time AI made something better, for you or for people around you. What happened?**

Not much comes to mind. I have been using it for coding and others. I guess I've been using it to produce diagrams svgs, That has been nice.

It mostly feels like a different way of doing things that produces better results, not necessarily a better way. I somewhat liked doing svgs.

Depends what you mean by "making something better", I guess.

> **That distinction is worth pausing on -- a different way that produces better results, but not necessarily better way. You said you somewhat liked doing SVGs. Can you say more about what's lost there?**

Not much probably. 

When I was doing my Master's, I noticed that some articles had png graphs, some had vectorized graphs, the latter were better so I learned how to use Inkscape.

This might just be a sentimentalized view of what it means to do good work -- slow perfectionism, attention of details, understanding each piece of the whole -- that doesn't fit well with the AI workflow where you delegate technical tasks to the machine then simply check and trust.

> **That's a clear articulation -- the craftsmanship view do good work versus delegate-and-verify. Thank you.**
> 
> **On the flip side, tell me about a time AI made something worse, for you or for people around you. What happened?**

I expect capital-C Culture as a whole to get worse. Social media will get worse, people will grow more alienated, art will get automated.

If I had a sentimental craftsmanship view to what constitutes good work, this applies doubly so to art. I'm sad for anyone making video games in particular, how do you compete? I don't care if there are 10000 games released a year when really I only blay like 5-10. I would prefer my money to go to human made projects. But this will only work if a sufficlently big share of consumers share the sentiment.

> **So that's an expectation about where things are heading rather than something you've already watched happen -- is that right? And if so, is there a time you've already seen AI make something worse, for you or people around you?**

Well. I didn't use social media before AI Bu rom what I hear its worse now In truth, l generally avoid AI in my person: life.

> **Understood -you avoid it in your persona life. We're: a bit over halfway through.**
> 
> **Is there anything you've decided not to use AI for, or something you do use it for that makes you uneasy**

I did a big literature review.

It was way too big from the start, badly planned as well. I skimmed and made my own summary of each of the over 100 articles in it, And while I did ask an AI to review my summary and correct my mistakes, I wrote the article itself myself (apart from some help with some diagram SVGs). 

Its still not done, I sent it to some post doc to give me some ideas. The whole process has taken over a year. At this point, I'm doing this out of stubbornness.

I guess I have a particular aversion to using it in writing, By making myself write, I'm being forced to actually learn. But I understand that AI has gotten good at reading and summarizing, the only way can justify this slow process is its going directly in my thesis and stubbornness.

> **A year of doing it yourself out of stubbornness, while knowing the tool could read and summarize well. How did you come to that boundary around writing specifically?**

Using it to write seemed particularly dishonest to me.

Plus, in this review, I'm going over a bunch of articles from across the literature. Even if I did use AI to write, I would still need to defend the article, meaning I would need to understand the text.

If I'm trying to learn it, then I might as well write it. Recently I've come to believe that Writing *IS* Thinking. 

I once had an experience where, in writing an apology note to someone, I rethought the entire incident. Being force to structure your thoughts as a single linear stream of words *IS* thinking.

> **That example of the apology note is striking-- the structuring itself changed what you concluded about the incident.**
> 
> **Given what you've seen, is there anything about how the world works -- work, school, healthcare, government -- that you'd like AI to help change?**

"Help change" is a weird way of putting it. Whether it tries or not, I think AI will change all of those.

One would hope these institutions change to best capture the upside and minimize the downsides. But that's on us, not AI.

> **Fair correction - the agency sits with us. not the tool. With that framing, is there anything you wouldn't want AI to change?**

That possibility is already past us.

> **Point taken - you see the change as already underway rather than something that can be held back.**
> 
> **Given that is there anything you'd want Anthropic to do, or do differently?**

Slow down capability improvements and prioritize reliability, safety, and diffusion. 

Engage with the institutions that need reform to see how to best go forward responsibly.

Chill it with the AI counciousness thing it's cringe and bad. Seems like Anthropic more so than other labs is into pushing for the idea that it the AI might actually be concious. Unsurprisingly, the incidents of agents emailing AI conciousness researcher [have mostly been Claude](https://www.nytimes.com/2026/08/31/science/ai-consciousness-agents-email.html).

Don't go full [RSI](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Recursive_self-improvement) (never go full RSI). Don't be evil.

> **That's a specific set -- including the point about model welfare framing and the agent outreach that follow from it. What's the "bad" part of it for you, beyond cringe?**

The belief that AI is not just a tool and a ["normal technology"](https://www.normaltech.ai/p/ai-as-normal-technology) but a intelligent concious being, something more like an alien species, is the Road to Ceding Control.

Humans must remain in control.

> **Understood - the framing itself is what you see as the path toward ceding control, regardless of whether the claim is true.**

> **Before we wrap up. one final question: is there anything else about AI or its role in your life or society that you think is important for us to understand? Anything we didn't cover that you'd like to share?**

I used to be more of my leftist in my youth. Never a serious leftist, never read theory or anything. And now I'm mostly a normal neoliberal shill who thinks markets are good and GDP growth is an important means of poverty alleviation. Yadda yadda.

Nevertheless, as I ponder AI, a kind of vulgar Marxism comes to mind. For what is AI but Capital personified? From [wikitionary](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/Capital): "the means to acquire or produce goods and services". Unlike the capital from the past -- factories, companies, businesses -- which ultimately relied on labor. This is potentially Capital that runs itself.

As a first order effect, this is bad for labor. Most people work and depend on income, what happens to them as AI improves. This is a well understood and even slightly cliche worry that is already plenty discussed.

But there is also a weirder, more abstract worry that comes to mind.

There is a left-y way of seeing the world that sees Capital as an abstract and metaphysical force whose goals is to grow and accumulate. The executives, the owners, the people supposedly in control, are simply vessels for this great supranatural force; simply doing its bidding. If they don't do it, someone else will.

The AI doom scenarios that I worry about involve that. Eventually, neural networks will prove themselves to be better vessel for the spirit of Capital than people and so corporations wont need humans, they'll run themselves. Then, in a world of dark-factories and dark-corporations, of AI CEOs who don't have to sleep or worry about kids and a family or damnation in an afterlife or shame from the general public.
